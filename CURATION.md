# Evidence curation

This repository is curated for reasoning, not for citation count.

A source is included because it constrains an important claim or mechanism. Journal prestige, citation count, and study-design labels are not sufficient reasons by themselves.

## Do not use one quality ladder

A randomized trial is strong evidence for a treatment comparison but usually weak evidence for a detailed mechanical mechanism. A small instrumented biomechanics experiment can be strong evidence that a measured quantity changes under a precisely defined loading protocol while saying almost nothing about long-term pain or disability.

Grade a source along separate axes:

- **role** — mechanism, measurement, clinical outcome, synthesis/guideline, historical framework;
- **directness** — how closely population, geometry, load, duration, and measured variable match the claim;
- **internal validity** — whether the design and measurement support the claimed result;
- **replication/triangulation** — whether independent methods or groups constrain the same claim;
- **generalisability** — how far the result can travel beyond the tested setup;
- **currency** — whether newer work materially changes the interpretation;
- **bias risk** — conflicts, selective reporting, weak controls, poor blinding where relevant, or model assumptions hidden as observations.

Do not convert these axes into one numerical score.

## Source roles

### Anchor

A source used to orient the corpus: systematic review/meta-analysis, major evidence-based guideline, or unusually comprehensive mechanics review.

Anchors are not automatically correct. Their job is to map a field, expose heterogeneity, and point to primary work.

### Mechanism

A primary human, animal, cadaver, isolated-tissue, or computational experiment that identifies a mechanical or physiological relationship under controlled conditions.

Mechanism sources require especially strict preservation of model, geometry, load, and time scale.

### Replication / constraint

An independent experiment or synthesis that tests the same relationship using another sample, method, direction, or time scale. These are important because a single elegant experiment can dominate a field for decades.

### Clinical outcome

Trials and longitudinal studies that test pain, function, recurrence, work absence, or other person-level outcomes. They should not be used to invent a mechanism that was not measured.

### Historical framework

Influential conceptual papers worth knowing because later literature uses their vocabulary. Historical importance is not evidence strength.

## Inclusion rule

For each important proposition, aim for a small evidence triangle rather than many redundant papers:

1. one modern synthesis or review when available;
2. one or two primary experiments that expose the measurement and loading details;
3. one independent replication, contrary result, or boundary case when available;
4. a clinical-outcome source only when the proposition is being connected to pain, function, recurrence, or work capacity.

A claim should not gain confidence merely because many papers reuse the same cohort, apparatus, model assumptions, or research group.

## Core lumbar corpus target

Do not attempt to ingest the whole low-back-pain literature.

A useful first stable core is roughly **30–40 sources**, distributed approximately as follows:

- 8–10 passive mechanics / load sharing / viscoelasticity;
- 5–7 disc hydration and load-history / diurnal mechanics;
- 5–7 active stiffness, intra-abdominal pressure, and motor control;
- 4–6 stretching, range-of-motion, and adaptation mechanisms;
- 5–7 pain-related kinematics and motor adaptation;
- 4–6 clinical syntheses on exercise, recurrence, and work/function.

These ranges overlap: one paper may serve more than one category. Stop adding papers when new papers cease to change the model or its uncertainty.

## Full text

Prefer legally redistributable full text for retrieval. For copyrighted work, store metadata plus an original bounded note. Do not let full-text availability determine evidentiary weight.

## Review cadence

When a modern systematic review exists, use its references to identify the primary experiments that actually determine the result. Record substantial heterogeneity, low-certainty ratings, and important negative findings rather than retaining only the review conclusion.

Every major model claim should eventually have a note stating:

- what was directly observed;
- what was calculated or modeled;
- what the authors inferred;
- what the repository infers;
- what would falsify or materially weaken that inference.
