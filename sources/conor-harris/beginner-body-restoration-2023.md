# Conor Harris — *Beginner Body Restoration* (2023)

## Source

- Author: Conor Harris
- Title: *Beginner Body Restoration*
- Date: 2023
- Format: self-published exercise-program eBook, 30 pages
- Scope: whole-body mobility and positioning, with particular emphasis on pelvis, ribcage, breathing, hip motion, trunk rotation, and asymmetrical exercise selection
- Relationship to PRI: several drills are explicitly credited to, or described as modifications of, Postural Restoration Institute (PRI) techniques

This note summarizes what the book says. It does **not** treat the book's mechanistic explanations as established evidence. The eBook is a practical program manual rather than a literature review, and it provides little primary-source support for its physiological and biomechanical claims.

## Main idea

Harris organizes the program around the claim that the pelvis and ribcage are foundational structures whose position and mobility constrain the motion available at the shoulders, spine, hips, knees, and feet. Rather than treating painful or restricted regions in isolation, he tries to change whole-body movement options by restoring positions he thinks are absent or difficult to access.

The program combines three recurring ideas:

1. **Position before force.** Harris prefers low-effort drills intended to make unfamiliar joint or trunk positions easier to access before loading them more aggressively.
2. **Breathing as a mechanical input.** Long, relaxed exhalations and quiet inhalations are used during many exercises to alter ribcage expansion, abdominal recruitment, spinal position, and perceived muscular effort.
3. **Asymmetry is expected.** Harris draws heavily on PRI's asymmetrical model, especially the "Left AIC" pattern. Some exercises are therefore deliberately unilateral rather than mirrored equally on both sides.

## Program structure

The program is six weeks long, divided into three two-week phases. Harris asks the reader to perform the drills in the order given and to spend at least 14 days on each phase before moving on. Sessions are generally prescribed three or more times per week, usually for three sets, with the option of splitting the work into morning and evening exposures.

He repeatedly emphasizes low effort. His suggested starting effort is roughly 3/10 and generally no more than 5/10. The stated rationale is that forcing a position recruits the very compensations the program is trying to reduce; gentle effort plus breathing is supposed to make the position feel less threatening and easier to retain.

### Breathing instructions

The breathing method is consistent throughout the book:

- relaxed, full exhalation through the mouth;
- no hard abdominal bracing;
- an attempt to feel the obliques rather than a strong rectus-abdominis contraction;
- roughly five-second exhalations and five-second quiet inhalations.

Harris treats breathing as both a respiratory exercise and a way to change trunk mechanics. This is central to the program, not an accessory cue.

## Phase 1: weeks 1–2

The first phase attempts to create more movement at the ribcage, spine, pelvis, and hips before introducing larger rotational tasks.

### Short-seated breathing

A modified PRI short-seated reach is used to expand the posterior ribcage and place the low and upper back in flexion. Harris recommends eight breaths and suggests a balloon or straw as an optional resistance tool.

### Sidelying scissor slices

This PRI exercise is intended to stretch the gluteal/posterior hip region and improve femoral internal rotation. The central idea is that the femur should be able to move posteriorly in the socket rather than obtaining apparent hip motion from the pelvis or lumbar spine.

### All-four breathing with ball push

This quadruped drill uses the lower abdominals and a posterior pelvic orientation while the person breathes into the back. Harris presents it as a way to reduce spinal compression and improve control of pelvic tilt.

### Supine hemi-extension with rectus femoris

This drill targets hip extension while trying to keep the lumbar spine relaxed. Harris recommends gradually reducing the amount of support/elevation as long as the low back remains quiet. The conceptual goal is to separate hip extension from lumbar extension and to let the two sides of the pelvis move more independently.

### TFL inhibition drill

The final phase-one drill is intended to reduce reliance on the tensor fasciae latae when hip mobility is limited. Harris tells the reader to choose the variation that produces the clearest gluteal sensation.

## Phase 2: weeks 3–4

The second phase adds more trunk rotation, shoulder motion, and explicit left-right asymmetry.

### Standing latissimus stretch

A PRI standing lat stretch is used to reduce what Harris describes as latissimus-related compression and to improve shoulder resting position and mobility.

### Seated posterior expansion with low reach

This drill emphasizes posterior thoracic expansion and shoulder motion. A unilateral modification is offered when one side of the back appears harder to expand.

### Short-kneeling alternating crossovers

This is a rotational drill intended to integrate trunk rotation with ribcage and shoulder motion.

### 90/90 hip lift with left hip shift

This is one of the clearest PRI-style asymmetrical drills in the book. Harris uses it specifically to improve left hip internal rotation, which he presents as a common limitation.

### Left sidelying right femoroacetabular abduction

This drill targets right hip external rotation and abduction, especially right gluteal recruitment. Harris instructs the reader to master the initial position before lifting the right foot away from its support.

## Phase 3: weeks 5–6

The final phase makes the earlier positions more dynamic and integrates them into larger movements.

### Same-side connect roll with hip extension

A rolling pattern links shoulder rotation, hip internal rotation, and hip extension. The exercise is progressed only after the easier variation is controlled.

### Modified wall stride

This drill emphasizes end-range hip extension, gluteal recruitment, and independent movement of the two sides of the pelvis.

### Left sidelying knee-toward-knee

This exercise combines right gluteal work with left hip internal rotation. Harris also uses maintenance of the right foot arch as a criterion for correct execution.

### Inchworm with alternating reach

The inchworm introduces calf length, ankle mobility, shoulder mobility, and a more global weight-bearing pattern.

### Bench/couch-supported lunge

The final exercise develops deep hip flexion and external rotation in a supported lunge position.

## What the book is actually trying to train

Despite the language of "restoration," the program can be understood more concretely as repeated exposure to several classes of motion:

- posterior ribcage expansion and trunk flexion during breathing;
- pelvic posterior tilt without excessive lumbar effort;
- hip internal rotation and posterior femoral motion;
- hip extension without substituting lumbar extension;
- trunk rotation;
- unilateral loading and left-right dissociation;
- right gluteal recruitment and left hip internal rotation in the PRI-influenced portions of the program.

The progression is therefore less mysterious than the branding suggests. It moves from supported, low-load positions toward larger and more integrated movements while trying to preserve the earlier trunk and hip mechanics.

## Claims that should not be imported uncritically

Several claims in the book are stated much more strongly than the evidence shown in the book itself supports. These should be treated as hypotheses or coaching models until checked against primary literature.

- The pelvis and ribcage are described as determining distal joint position in a fairly global way. Mechanical coupling is real, but the strength and direction of those effects are task-specific and should not be generalized without measurement.
- The "Left AIC" pattern is treated as broadly applicable, including to people who do not initially believe they fit it. That is a PRI-specific explanatory model and should be separated from directly observed asymmetry.
- Statements that more frequent exposure will make the body "accept new positions" are plausible as motor-learning language but are not quantified here.
- The sympathetic/parasympathetic explanation for why hard effort interferes with positional training is presented without evidence in the eBook. The practical observation that high effort changes recruitment strategy is more defensible than the book's autonomic explanation.
- The breathing section states that the obliques "drive proper respiration" and suggests that failure to feel them means the exhale is too short. That is a coaching cue, not a demonstrated diagnostic test.
- Terms such as "decompression," "inhibition," and "neutral" are used functionally rather than operationally. The book does not supply pressure, joint-force, imaging, EMG, or kinematic measurements demonstrating these effects.

## Useful parts for this repository

The book is useful here primarily as a **map of a contemporary mechanics-based treatment model**. It supplies testable propositions rather than strong evidence by itself. Useful follow-up questions include:

- How much can breathing measurably change thoracic and lumbar geometry in these positions?
- Does a posterior pelvic orientation during the drills actually alter lumbar compression or only change subjective muscular effort?
- How coupled are hip internal rotation, foot mechanics, and pelvic rotation under load?
- Can the unilateral PRI drills produce persistent changes in gait or only short-term changes in range of motion and sensation?
- Which effects depend on the exact starting posture, and which generalize to standing and walking?
- Are the claimed asymmetries reproducible across observers and sessions?

For the repository's purposes, the exercises are best treated as interventions whose geometry and loading can be described precisely, while the explanatory labels should be tested separately.
