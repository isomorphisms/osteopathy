# Conor Harris — *Lower Limb Foundations* (2024 edition)

## Source

- Author: Conor Harris
- Title: *Lower Limb Foundations*
- Supplied edition: 2024
- Copyright notice in the PDF: 2023–present
- Format: self-published exercise-program eBook, 59 pages
- Scope: foot, ankle, knee, hip, and trunk mechanics during gait and athletic movement; six-week progression combining mobility, isometrics, strength work, and plyometrics
- Relationship to PRI and other coaches: several exercises are explicitly credited to the Postural Restoration Institute; others are credited to David Grey, Andrew Hauser, Dave O'Sullivan, Zac Cupples, and Paul Fabritz

This note summarizes Harris's model and program. It does **not** treat the book as primary evidence. The eBook contains many biomechanical claims but very little direct citation of research literature, so its mechanisms should be checked separately against experimental gait, foot, tendon, and joint-mechanics work.

## Main idea

Harris's central claim is that the foot cannot be understood as an isolated structure. He treats the foot, tibia, femur, pelvis, trunk, and upper body as a coupled system that moves through recurring phases during gait. When one joint or segment lacks a motion, he expects some other segment to provide a compensatory substitute.

The program is therefore built around **restoring transitions**, not simply strengthening one muscle or raising/lowering an arch. In Harris's model, useful lower-limb function requires the person to be able to move between:

- relative external rotation and internal rotation;
- foot supination and pronation;
- heel contact, foot-flat, mid-stance, and late-stance positions;
- compliant/lengthening behavior and stiff/co-contracting behavior;
- isolated positional drills and faster, more dynamic tasks.

The six-week program gradually moves from controlled mobility work into isometric, strength, and plyometric work intended to make those positions usable under load and speed.

## Harris's gait model

### Heel strike and early stance

Harris describes initial contact as a lateral-heel strike with the hip flexed and relatively externally rotated. After contact, the lower limb is supposed to move rapidly toward internal rotation while the foot progresses toward pronation.

He describes the foot at initial contact as relatively supinated and plantar-flexed, with early stance ending when the great toe contacts the ground.

### Foot flat

During foot flat, Harris expects the arch to lower and the lower limb to continue toward internal rotation. He describes the pelvis as turned away from the stance side during this phase.

He links arch lowering to loading of the plantar structures so that the foot can subsequently become stiffer and re-form the arch later in stance. The book calls this the "Windlass Mechanism." That terminology should be checked carefully against standard foot-biomechanics definitions; the eBook uses it broadly for the loading/re-springing sequence rather than documenting the mechanism experimentally.

### Mid-stance

In Harris's account, the body begins to turn back toward the stance side as the arch rises toward supination. The hip can still be internally rotated while the foot is beginning to re-supinate. He treats this combination as important because it demonstrates that the foot and hip need not switch rotational state at exactly the same instant.

Mid-stance ends, in his description, when the heel starts to rise.

### Late stance and toe-off

Late stance is characterized by a relatively high/supinated arch and a transition toward external rotation as the body leaves the stance limb. Harris repeatedly argues that effective late stance depends on having passed through usable internal rotation and pronation earlier in stance.

This produces the program's overall sequence: first gain early-stance options, then mid-stance control, then late-stance propulsion.

## Compensation model

Harris treats missing range of motion as something the body will obtain elsewhere "through the path of least resistance." The most prominent example is a forward-shifted or anteriorly tilted pelvis, which he says can move the center of mass toward the forefoot and reduce access to heel strike, hip extension, and external rotation.

He also emphasizes mismatched rotations between bones. A recurring example is a femur that appears internally rotated while the tibia remains externally rotated and the foot pronates. Harris interprets this as a torsional compensation rather than a simple, unified lower-limb rotation.

The useful point for this repository is not that every such posture has one cause, but that **segmental rotations should be measured separately**. Femoral orientation, tibial rotation, calcaneal position, arch deformation, and pelvic rotation can vary independently enough that a single label such as "pronated" or "internally rotated" may hide the actual geometry.

## Walking versus running and faster movement

Harris distinguishes walking from running and sport rather than treating them as the same gait at different speeds. His main distinction is that faster movement requires larger and faster co-contractions around joints so that tendons and other elastic tissues can store and return energy.

The program therefore includes not only mobility and strength work but also isometrics and plyometrics. Harris presents those as necessary for tissue capacity and for learning to stabilize joints under faster loading.

## Breathing

Breathing remains part of this lower-limb program, though less centrally than in *Beginner Body Restoration*. On drills that use it, Harris again prescribes relaxed oral exhalations and quiet inhalations, generally lasting 5–10 seconds each. He wants the reader to feel the obliques rather than a hard anterior abdominal brace.

The mechanical purpose is to coordinate ribcage and pelvic position with foot contact and lower-limb motion. As in the earlier book, the physiological explanation is largely asserted rather than demonstrated.

## Program structure

The six weeks are divided into three phases:

- **Weeks 1–2:** establish early-stance mechanics, relative external rotation, heel contact, and the ability to move toward pronation/internal rotation.
- **Weeks 3–4:** emphasize pronation, dorsiflexion, internal rotation, and more demanding unilateral control.
- **Weeks 5–6:** integrate those positions into upright, gait-like, strength, isometric, and plyometric tasks that bias the transition toward late stance.

The program uses four categories of work:

1. **Daily drills** — positional/mobility exercises performed repeatedly, often every day.
2. **Isometric exercises** — longer holds aimed at joint co-contraction and tendon/joint loading.
3. **Strength exercises** — slow or controlled loaded movements, usually three times per week.
4. **Plyometric exercises** — hops and faster contacts intended to expose the ankle, knee, and foot to higher-rate loading.

Harris recommends keeping strength work around an RPE of 8/10 or less, usually leaving about two good repetitions in reserve.

## Weeks 1–2: early-stance foundation

### Standing supported posterior hip capsule stretch

This drill is intended to improve hip internal rotation while keeping weight mostly through the heel. Harris cues the stretch to be felt in the posterior/lateral hip and not at the front of the hip.

The conceptual role is to create enough posterior hip motion to allow the femur to internally rotate without substituting from the pelvis or spine.

### Tibial untwister

This is a slow rotational drill intended to improve tibial internal rotation while the knee remains aligned with the foot and hip. Harris emphasizes maintaining three points of foot contact: heel, base of the big toe, and base of the little toe.

This is one of the book's most direct attempts to separate tibial rotation from collapse of the foot or uncontrolled knee deviation.

### Wedge supination drill

A wedge is used under the foot while the person turns the pelvis toward the stance side. Harris expects the arch to rise slightly while the three points of contact remain on the floor. Low-back stiffening is treated as evidence that the person has rotated farther than the hip/foot system can currently manage.

### Bilateral hamstring isometric bridge

The first isometric drill uses a bridge to build prolonged co-contraction around the lower limb. Harris progresses the duration toward 45 seconds.

### Hamstring bridge with tibial internal-rotation focus

This strength exercise couples hip extension with controlled tibial rotation. It is credited to David Grey. The movement is deliberately slow, using a 3:1:3 tempo.

### Seated tempo calf raise

A slow calf raise strengthens the calf while the knee is held in a flexed position. Harris also describes the position as reducing posterior knee compression, a claim that would need direct mechanical support before being treated as established.

### Bilateral pogo hops and supine wall switches

The phase ends with low-level plyometric work. Harris uses these exercises to introduce tendon loading and faster co-contraction without yet requiring complex single-leg control.

## Weeks 3–4: pronation and internal-rotation control

### Pronation drill

This drill combines forward knee travel, ankle dorsiflexion, and subtle arch lowering. Harris again insists on maintaining heel, first-metatarsal, and fifth-metatarsal contact.

This is a useful operational feature of the program: pronation is not coached as simply collapsing medially. The reader is asked to lower the arch while preserving distributed plantar contact.

### 90/90 hip lift with ball hold

This PRI-style drill uses the hamstrings and abdominals to control pelvic orientation while the back remains relaxed. It is intended to reduce compensatory lumbar effort and provide a better proximal position for lower-limb motion.

### Sidelying grounding with breathing focus

The foot presses into a wall while the trunk and pelvis are supported in sidelying. Harris uses the drill to coordinate plantar pressure with abdominal/ribcage position.

### Hauser pronation isometric

This exercise, credited to Andrew Hauser, holds a pronation-biased position for 30–45 seconds. Harris describes it as strengthening the musculature that supports controlled pronation and notes that some people immediately feel the foot more connected to the floor afterward.

The immediate subjective change is an observation, not proof of the proposed mechanism.

### Single-leg isometric hamstring bridge

The bilateral bridge progresses to a unilateral hold intended to increase co-contraction among the hamstring, gluteal, and calf musculature.

### Heel-float split squat

This slow split squat loads the calf, quadriceps, and hamstrings while the rear heel remains elevated. Support from a table or doorframe is allowed to reduce the balance and strength demand.

### Single-leg box pogo hops

The bilateral hops progress to unilateral contacts, increasing the demand on tendon stiffness, balance, and lower-limb co-contraction.

## Weeks 5–6: transition toward late stance

### Sidelying glute max

This drill coordinates gluteal recruitment with pressure through the foot arch against a wall. Harris uses it to couple pelvic rotation with foot contact rather than training the glute in isolation.

### Alternating reciprocal step-through with elevation

A PRI exercise is used to simulate the transition from mid- to late stance. It is more upright and gait-like than the earlier positional drills.

### Spring-ankle isometric

This prolonged plantar-flexed/loaded stance is intended to strengthen plantar-foot musculature and co-contraction around the foot, knee, and hip. Harris recommends it daily despite classifying it as an isometric.

### Single-leg hamstring bridge with oscillation

The unilateral bridge gains a small dynamic perturbation so the lower limb must maintain co-contraction while the load changes.

### Bottoms-up split squat with pronation focus

Credited to Zac Cupples, this isometric split-squat variation targets the transition from mid- to late stance while loading the gluteus maximus, quadriceps, and hip abductors.

### Kickstand hinge with forefoot focus

This movement adds a more dynamic hip hinge while maintaining forefoot loading. Harris describes it as integrating calf, hamstring, quadriceps, and gluteal co-contraction.

### Single-leg step-down progression

The step-down is performed slowly, with a 5:1:3 tempo, to train lower-limb internal rotation and controlled lengthening of the muscles surrounding the knee.

### Multi-directional pogo hops and lateral hurdle hops

The final plyometric work introduces direction changes, single-leg loading, fatigue, and higher force production. By this point the program has moved far from passive mobility and into repeated elastic loading.

## Footwear and wedges

Harris devotes a section to footwear because he considers the shoe part of the mechanical environment. He argues against both excessive cushioning and insufficient support as universal solutions. In particular, he notes that a person with a high, relatively rigid arch may not benefit from an extremely minimal shoe if the arch cannot lower toward the floor.

His practical goal is eventually to tolerate a wide range of footwear, but he allows temporary support while the person develops more movement options.

Wedges are used as external constraints to bias specific foot positions. This is mechanically important: the wedge changes the ground plane under part of the foot and therefore changes the moments and available contact pressures. It should not be treated as merely a proprioceptive cue.

## What is most useful mechanically

Several ideas in the book are worth preserving independently of Harris's broader explanatory model:

- Separate the rotations of pelvis, femur, tibia, rearfoot, and forefoot instead of assuming they move as one rigid chain.
- Treat pronation and supination as dynamic states with load, timing, and plantar-contact distribution, not simply as static arch height.
- Measure transitions between phases, not only endpoint range of motion.
- Distinguish the mechanics of walking, running, hopping, and slow strength work.
- Recognize that the same apparent joint angle can occur under very different muscle forces and external loads.
- Progress from low-load access to loaded control and then to faster elastic tasks if the actual goal includes running or athletic movement.
- Treat shoes and wedges as boundary conditions that change the mechanics of the task.

## Claims that need primary-source checking

The book contains many statements that are plausible as coaching heuristics but too broad to accept without qualification.

- The proposed sequence linking pelvic rotation, femoral rotation, tibial rotation, pronation, and supination is presented as typical but is not quantified. Actual coupling varies substantially between people, tasks, speed, and footwear.
- The statement that people with foot and knee pain "almost always" have significant hip or trunk limitations is much stronger than the evidence supplied in the book.
- The description of the windlass mechanism should be checked against primary foot-biomechanics literature; Harris uses the term in a broader loading/recoil sense than the classical great-toe/plantar-fascia formulation.
- "Decompression" is used without direct measurements of joint contact force, pressure, or tissue stress.
- Co-contraction is treated as generally desirable for faster movement, but excessive co-contraction can also raise joint loading and energy cost. The useful amount is task-dependent.
- Claims that particular isometrics preferentially strengthen "pronation musculature" or specific plantar tissues need EMG, force, or modeling evidence if they are to be stated anatomically rather than used as exercise intent.
- The suggested relationships between anterior pelvic tilt, center of mass, heel strike, and foot mechanics require task-specific measurement rather than inference from static posture.

## Questions for the osteopathy corpus

This book generates a useful set of empirical questions:

- How much coupling is actually observed among pelvic rotation, femoral rotation, tibial rotation, calcaneal eversion/inversion, and medial-arch deformation during walking?
- At what points in stance do those segmental rotations change direction, and how variable is the timing between subjects?
- Does increasing passive hip internal rotation alter foot pronation during gait, or are those variables only weakly coupled?
- How much do wedges change subtalar motion, plantar pressure, tibial rotation, knee moments, and hip rotation?
- Which changes produced by these drills persist after the external constraint is removed?
- How do slow isometrics, slow strength work, and plyometrics differ in tendon strain, tendon stiffness, muscle activation, and joint contact force?
- What does a "high arch" or "low arch" predict dynamically, if anything, once load, speed, and footwear are controlled?
- Can segmental compensation be measured as a redistribution of motion between joints rather than inferred from appearance?

For this repository, *Lower Limb Foundations* is most useful as a detailed statement of one contemporary whole-chain movement model. It should be mined for testable geometric and loading claims, with the practical exercise sequence kept separate from the evidentiary status of the explanations offered for it.
